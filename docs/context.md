# Sesiidae, Lipid-Derived Signaling, and the Amegilla Connection

**Companion context document for the Sesiidae Pheromone Atlas**

**Purpose:** preserve the natural-history motivation, the biological big picture, the established facts about Sesiidae that motivate the project, and the conceptual link from *Amegilla* / blue-banded bee taxonomy to lipid-derived chemical signaling.

---

## 1. Why this document exists

The **Sesiidae Pheromone Atlas** technical concept describes *what to build*: a curated, machine-readable resource connecting species, pheromone chemistry, genomes, candidate pathways, and enzyme families.

This document describes **why this is scientifically and personally interesting in the first place**.

The project did not begin from a generic interest in pheromone databases. It emerged from a sequence of natural-history questions:

```text
bee taxonomy
    ↓
Amegilla / blue-banded bees
    ↓
morphological variation and recognition
    ↓
bee-like clearwing moths
    ↓
Sesiidae mimicry
    ↓
Sesiidae pheromone chemistry
    ↓
unusual C18 lipid-derived signals
    ↓
how does biology generate and discriminate
closely related molecular structures?
```

That sequence is already visible in the original project slides (`sessidae pheromone_1.pdf`):

- page 1: **Bee taxonomy lecture (January 2024)**
- page 2 onward: **Blue-banded bee (*Amegilla*, Anthophorini)**
- subsequent pages: taxonomic variation in *Amegilla*
- page 11: a bee-like clearwing moth and characteristic Sesiidae pheromone structures
- page 12: the general lipid-derived moth pheromone biosynthetic logic

The Sesiidae project is therefore not separate from the *Amegilla* work. It is a branch that grew from the same interest in **how organisms encode identity, recognition, and ecological relationships in phenotype**—first through morphology and behavior, and now through chemistry.

# 2. The Amegilla starting point

## 2.1 Blue-banded bees as the original natural-history system

The initial interest was *Amegilla*, especially blue-banded bees in the tribe Anthophorini.

The work developed around:

- taxonomy and species identification;
- museum specimens;
- variation in abdominal banding and pubescence;
- apical tergites;
- male facial markings;
- geographic variation;
- large observational datasets;
- the problem of deciding which visual characters actually carry taxonomic information.

An important recurring lesson from *Amegilla* is:

> **Similarity is not identity.**

Two bees may share the same broad blue-banded phenotype while differing in subtle but repeatable characters. Conversely, one species may vary substantially across sex, geography, age, or preservation state.

This is already a useful conceptual precursor to pheromone biology.

A chemical signal also cannot be reduced to:

> “these two molecules look similar.”

Biological identity may depend on:

- a double-bond position;
- E versus Z geometry;
- alcohol versus acetate versus aldehyde;
- blend ratio;
- minor components;
- antagonists;
- time of release;
- environmental and behavioral context.

The taxonomic habit of looking for **which differences actually matter** transfers naturally from morphology to molecular signaling.

# 3. How Sesiidae entered the picture

## 3.1 Clearwing moths look like Hymenoptera

Sesiidae, the clearwing moths, are a worldwide family of Lepidoptera. A recent Korean synthesis lists more than **1,563 species in 171 genera**, with especially high diversity in the Afrotropical and Oriental realms.

Typical adult traits include:

- partly or largely transparent wings;
- reduced wing scaling;
- diurnal activity in many species;
- body shapes and color patterns resembling bees or wasps;
- behavioral traits that reinforce this resemblance.

Their larvae are usually internal borers in:

- trunks;
- stems;
- branches;
- roots;
- fruits or other plant tissues.

Many species are therefore important in agriculture and forestry.

**Reference:**  
*Integrated Identification and Genetic Diversity of Potentially Invasive Clearwing Moths in Korea* (2024).  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10816279/

## 3.2 Sesiidae are not merely visually bee-like

Clearwing moth mimicry can extend beyond static morphology.

Experimental and field work in Southeast Asian Sesiidae has shown:

- bee-like body form;
- bee-like or wasp-like flight trajectories;
- mud-puddling alongside Hymenoptera;
- in some cases, acoustic resemblance.

For example, *Heterosphecia pahangensis*, *Aschistophleps argentifasciata*, and *Pyrophleps cruentata* show flight trajectories resembling sympatric bees.

**Reference:**  
Skowron Volponi et al. (2018), *Moving like a model: mimicry of hymenopteran flight trajectories by clearwing moths of Southeast Asian rainforests*.  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6012703/

A Thai clearwing moth, *Aschistophleps argentifasciata*, was explicitly described as a bee mimic whose behavior reinforces its morphology.

**Reference:**  
Skowron Volponi & Volponi (2018), *A new species of bee-mimicking clearwing moth (Lepidoptera: Sesiidae) from Thailand, with description and video of its behaviour*.  
DOI: 10.1016/j.aspen.2017.12.007

A later study also tested acoustic mimicry in Southeast Asian clearwings and included **Amegilla sp.** among the bee comparisons.

**Reference:**  
Skowron Volponi et al. (2021), *Southeast Asian clearwing moths buzz like their model bees*.  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8262067/

# 4. What exactly is the Amegilla–Sesiidae relationship?

This needs careful wording.

## What we can say

The natural-history connection is strong:

1. *Amegilla* was the original study system.
2. The conspicuous morphology of blue-banded and other large bees made bee mimicry in clearwing moths immediately interesting.
3. Sesiidae include many remarkable Hymenoptera mimics.
4. Southeast Asian Sesiidae inhabit landscapes containing diverse bees, including anthophorine bees such as *Amegilla*.
5. *Amegilla* has even been used as a comparison taxon in work on Sesiidae acoustic mimicry.
6. Studying bee-like Sesiidae naturally opened the question of how these moths recognize **one another**, rather than how predators recognize them.
7. That leads directly to sex pheromones.

## What we should **not** claim

We should not currently write that a particular Sesiidae species has been demonstrated to mimic a particular *Amegilla* species unless a study establishes it.

The safest framing is:

> **generalized bee / large-bee / Amegilla-class resemblance was the natural-history bridge that sparked the project.**

The mimicry literature often identifies other local bee models more directly—for example stingless bees in *Heterosphecia* studies.

So the Atlas should preserve the *Amegilla* relationship primarily as:

- project origin;
- comparative natural history;
- a route into Sesiidae;
- a shared question about biological recognition.

It should not manufacture an unsupported one-to-one mimic–model assignment.

# 5. A deeper bridge: Amegilla also uses lipid-derived chemical information

There is also a real biochemical/ecological connection, although it is **not evidence of a homologous pheromone pathway**.

In *Amegilla dawsoni* (Dawson's burrowing bee), cuticular hydrocarbon blends influence sexual attraction.

Experiments showed that:

- removing the cuticular hydrocarbon blend from emerging virgin females made them unattractive to searching males;
- restoring the blend restored attractiveness;
- male, virgin-female, and nesting-female hydrocarbon profiles differ.

**Reference:**  
Simmons, Alcock & Reeder (2003), *The role of cuticular hydrocarbons in male attraction and repulsion by female Dawson's burrowing bee, Amegilla dawsoni*.  
Animal Behaviour 66:677–685.  
DOI: 10.1006/anbe.2003.2240

This is useful conceptually because insect cuticular hydrocarbons are also **lipid-derived molecules**.

Their ancestral / physiological roles include waterproofing and protection against desiccation, but the same chemical space has repeatedly been recruited for communication.

This yields a broader comparative question:

> **How does insect lipid metabolism become a vocabulary for biological identity?**

In Sesiidae, the vocabulary is dominated by specialized volatile fatty-acid-derived sex pheromones.

In *Amegilla dawsoni*, surface hydrocarbon composition can contribute to sexual recognition.

These are not the same system, but they belong to a larger evolutionary pattern in which modifications of lipid metabolism generate signals that other insects evolve to read.

# 6. Lipids as a general signaling substrate in insects

Lipid metabolism provides biology with several tunable molecular dimensions:

```text
carbon-chain length
double-bond position
double-bond geometry
number of unsaturations
methyl branching
oxidation state
terminal functional group
esterification
relative abundance
```

Small enzymatic changes can therefore produce chemically related but biologically distinguishable molecules.

For signaling, this is powerful.

A lineage does not have to invent an entirely new biosynthetic pathway to change its signal. It can change:

- which precursor is used;
- which carbon is desaturated;
- E versus Z stereochemistry;
- how many elongation or shortening cycles occur;
- which fatty-acyl substrate is reduced;
- whether an alcohol remains an alcohol or becomes an aldehyde or acetate.

This provides a large molecular vocabulary using a relatively small number of enzyme families.

---

# 7. Cuticular hydrocarbons: metabolism becomes identity

Insect cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs) illustrate this principle particularly clearly.

CHCs originally serve an essential physiological role:

> restricting water loss through the insect cuticle.

But they have also evolved into signals involved in:

- species recognition;
- sex recognition;
- mate choice;
- fertility;
- nestmate recognition;
- dominance;
- social organization.

Their biosynthesis is tied to fatty-acid metabolism:

```text
acetyl-CoA / malonyl-CoA
        ↓
fatty-acid synthesis
        ↓
elongation
        ↓
optional desaturation / branching
        ↓
fatty-acyl reduction
        ↓
aldehyde
        ↓
oxidative decarbonylation
        ↓
hydrocarbon
```

Thus, chain length, unsaturation and branching can simultaneously reflect metabolism, physical properties, and signaling function.

**Reference:**  
Blomquist & Ginzel (2021), *Chemical Ecology, Biochemistry, and Molecular Biology of Insect Hydrocarbons*.  
DOI: 10.1146/annurev-ento-031620-071754

# 8. Type-I moth pheromones: a specialized lipid signaling language

Most familiar moth sex pheromones belong to the **Type-I** class.

They are typically:

- straight-chain;
- C10–C18;
- unsaturated;
- fatty-acid derived;
- terminated as alcohols, aldehydes, or acetate esters.

A useful generic pathway is:

```text
acetyl-CoA
    ↓
malonyl-CoA
    ↓
fatty-acid synthesis
    ↓
C16 / C18 saturated fatty acyl precursor
    ↓
desaturation
    ↓
chain shortening and/or elongation
    ↓
fatty-acyl reduction
    ↓
alcohol
   ↙   ↘
oxidation  acetylation
   ↓         ↓
aldehyde   acetate
```

Important enzyme families include:

- acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC);
- fatty acid synthase (FAS);
- fatty-acyl desaturases (FAD/DES);
- elongases (ELO);
- β-oxidation machinery;
- fatty-acyl reductases (FAR);
- alcohol oxidases / dehydrogenases;
- acetyltransferases;
- esterases and related terminal-modification enzymes.

**References:**

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6964838/
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8632438/
- Löfstedt et al. (2016), *The Genetic Basis of Pheromone Evolution in Moths*.  
  DOI: 10.1146/annurev-ento-010715-023638

# 9. Why desaturases are especially interesting

Fatty-acyl desaturases convert ordinary lipid precursors into highly informative signaling scaffolds.

They control dimensions such as:

```text
Δ position
E / Z geometry
substrate chain length
single versus multiple desaturations
```

Moth desaturases evolved from enzymes that originally participated in ordinary membrane-lipid metabolism.

Gene duplication, functional divergence, and regulatory changes allowed some copies to specialize in pheromone biosynthesis.

This is one of the most elegant aspects of the system:

> **a housekeeping lipid enzyme family becomes an engine for species-specific communication.**

**Reference:**  
*Evolution of the integral membrane desaturase gene family in moths and flies*.  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1462379/

# 10. Chemical similarity does not imply biological equivalence

This is one of the main reasons Sesiidae are so attractive.

Consider molecules that differ only by:

- Δ2 versus Δ3;
- E versus Z;
- OH versus OAc;
- OH versus CHO.

To a beginner, these can look nearly interchangeable.

To a moth, they may mean:

```text
mate
wrong species
antagonist
irrelevant background
```

This creates a natural mechanistic question:

> **Where does specificity come from?**

Possible layers include:

```text
substrate chemistry
    ↓
enzyme substrate selection
    ↓
enzyme regioselectivity
    ↓
stereoselectivity
    ↓
pheromone blend
    ↓
release timing
    ↓
receptor recognition
    ↓
behavior
```

The Sesiidae Atlas can focus initially on the **biosynthetic half** of this chain.

# 11. Sesiidae pheromone chemistry: a recurring C18 theme

A striking part of known Sesiidae chemistry is the recurrence of **C18 2,13- and 3,13-diene derivatives**.

Representative structures include:

```text
E2,Z13-18:OAc
E3,Z13-18:OAc
Z3,Z13-18:OAc
E3,Z13-18:OH
Z3,Z13-18:OH
E2,Z13-18:Ald
```

The same basic hydrocarbon scaffold can therefore encode biological differences through:

- double-bond position;
- alkene geometry;
- terminal group;
- blend composition.

This is an unusually clean system for asking how enzymes create chemically subtle but behaviorally important diversity.

# 12. Representative Sesiidae pheromone cases

## 12.1 Synanthedon tipuliformis

Female gland extracts include:

- `E2,Z13-18:OAc` — dominant;
- `E3,Z13-18:OAc` — minor;
- `Z13-18:OAc`;
- `E2,Z13-18:OH`;
- trace `Z13-18:OH`;
- trace `18:OH`.

A study reported approximately:

```text
E2,Z13-18:OAc : E3,Z13-18:OAc
100 : 0.7
```

Yet the minor E3 component strongly increased attraction relative to the major component alone.

**Reference:**  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16729590/

## 12.2 Synanthedon scoliaeformis

This species also uses:

```text
E2,Z13-18:OAc
```

Specificity relative to *S. tipuliformis* involves:

- minor compounds;
- reciprocal antagonism;
- different daily mate-searching periods.

**Reference:**  
PMID: 23963807

## 12.3 Synanthedon bicingulata

Major components:

```text
E3,Z13-18:OAc
Z3,Z13-18:OAc
```

approximately:

```text
4 : 6
```

Both are important for attraction.

**Reference:**  
DOI: 10.1007/s10886-011-9929-0  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21399964/

## 12.4 Synanthedon tenuis

Principal female-produced pheromone:

```text
Z3,Z13-18:OH
```

The corresponding acetate was not detected in female glands in the Korean study and strongly inhibited attraction when added.

**Reference:**  
DOI: 10.1007/s10886-012-0179-6  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22976589/

## 12.5 Nokona pernix

Female components:

```text
E3,Z13-18:OH
Z3,Z13-18:OH
```

approximately:

```text
9 : 1
```

The blend was much more attractive than either component alone.

**Reference:**  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16495670/

## 12.6 Sesia apiformis

The hornet moth uses a blend including:

```text
Z3,Z13-18:OH
E2,Z13-18:Ald
```

reported at approximately 2:3 in pooled gland extracts.

Neither component alone was strongly attractive; the mixture was highly active.

**Reference:**  
DOI: 10.1023/B:JOEC.0000028433.67396.93  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15260225/

# 13. The signal is a system, not just a molecule

The Sesiidae examples suggest a useful hierarchy:

```text
molecular identity
    ↓
blend composition
    ↓
release dynamics / timing
    ↓
receptor sensitivity
    ↓
behavior
    ↓
species isolation
```

Important dimensions include:

- presence / absence;
- abundance;
- ratio;
- synergism;
- antagonism;
- flight period;
- time of day;
- geographic population;
- physiological state.

This is one reason the Atlas should store **pheromone observations as first-class records**, instead of assigning a single static pheromone list to each species.

# 14. Why the same chemistry can support both attraction and isolation

The *S. tipuliformis* / *S. scoliaeformis* system is especially instructive.

Both use the same dominant compound:

```text
E2,Z13-18:OAc
```

Yet specificity emerges through:

- a small secondary component;
- reciprocal antagonism;
- different activity periods.

A useful general principle is:

> **Biological specificity can be encoded combinatorially rather than by a chemically unique molecule.**

# 16. Why RNA-seq is desirable but not required

Pheromone-gland transcriptomics would improve candidate ranking by identifying genes that are:

- expressed in females;
- expressed in the pheromone gland;
- temporally associated with pheromone production.

But the absence of such data should not stop the project.

For an enigmatic family such as Sesiidae, the combination:

```text
credible pheromone profile
+
chromosome-scale genome
+
comparative enzyme knowledge from other Lepidoptera
```

is already a powerful starting point.

The project should distinguish:

```text
candidate from genome + phylogeny
```

from:

```text
pheromone-gland-expressed candidate
```

from:

```text
functionally characterized enzyme
```

rather than treating the first category as inadequate.

# 17. The central unresolved biological question

For many of the most interesting Sesiidae molecules, we know the **signal structure** but not the **biosynthetic enzyme sequence**.

Questions include:

- How is the Z13 double bond formed?
- Is it introduced directly at Δ13 in a C18 precursor?
- Does it arise through Δ11 desaturation in C16 followed by elongation?
- How are Δ2 and Δ3 unsaturations introduced?
- Are E3 and Z3 generated by separate enzymes or a multifunctional enzyme?
- What is the actual substrate state?
- At which stage are reduction, oxidation, and acetylation performed?
- Which enzyme-family duplications correlate with unusual chemistry?

These are exactly the questions the Atlas should make tractable.

---

# 18. One major lesson from other moths: the obvious pathway may be wrong

A double bond at position 13 in a C18 product does not necessarily imply a Δ13 desaturase acting directly on C18.

Related Lepidoptera demonstrate that final pheromone structures can arise through combinations of:

```text
desaturation
+
elongation
+
chain shortening
+
terminal-group conversion
```

Therefore, Sesiidae pathway reconstruction should begin with **alternative reaction graphs**, not annotation-name searches.

For example:

```text
Route A

18:0
  ↓ direct Δ13 desaturation
Z13-18
```

versus:

```text
Route B

16:0
  ↓ Δ11 desaturation
Z11-16
  ↓ +C2 elongation
Z13-18
```

The genome search follows only after these alternatives are explicit.

# 19. Why this is a good system for learning organic chemistry

The molecules are structurally simple enough to draw but biologically rich enough to challenge naive chemical intuition.

They naturally introduce:

- carbon numbering;
- alkene geometry;
- E/Z stereochemistry;
- alcohols;
- aldehydes;
- esters;
- oxidation state;
- fatty-acid derivatives;
- conformational flexibility;
- regioselectivity;
- stereoselectivity;
- enzyme specificity.

That makes Sesiidae chemistry unusually suitable for connecting textbook organic chemistry with real biology.

# 22. From mimicry to private recognition

There is also an appealing ecological contrast.

Sesiidae adults may advertise a **public visual phenotype** that resembles Hymenoptera:

```text
predator sees:
"bee / wasp-like"
```

Yet conspecific moths still need a **private communication channel**:

```text
mate detects:
specific pheromone blend
```

So a single animal participates in two recognition systems:

```text
visual / behavioral resemblance
    → deceive another species

chemical specificity
    → identify its own species
```

The animal simultaneously benefits from:

- **similarity** to its mimicry model;
- **specificity** toward its mate.

A concise way to express the tension is:

> **look like something else, smell like yourself.**

# 23. Multimodality is part of the story

Clearwing moths can involve:

- visual morphology;
- locomotor behavior;
- acoustic cues;
- chemical sex signals.

Different channels solve different problems.

| Channel | Possible function |
|---|---|
| morphology | predator deception |
| flight behavior | reinforce mimicry |
| sound | reinforce hymenopteran resemblance / other functions |
| sex pheromone | mate attraction and species recognition |
| timing | further isolate signaling channels |

No single modality should automatically be treated as sufficient.

# 24. Why lipid signaling is evolutionarily attractive

Lipid metabolism is ancient and universal.

That means signaling evolution can often proceed by modifying existing metabolic machinery rather than inventing new chemistry from scratch.

Potential evolutionary moves include:

```text
gene duplication
↓
changed substrate preference
↓
changed regiospecificity
↓
changed stereoselectivity
↓
new tissue expression
↓
new blend ratio
↓
new communication phenotype
```

A mutation affecting one enzyme can therefore shift a signal in chemically interpretable ways.

This provides a tractable genotype → chemistry → behavior chain.

# 26. What we currently know versus what remains open

## Relatively strong

- Sesiidae are a diverse worldwide clearwing-moth family.
- Bee/wasp mimicry is widespread and can extend to behavior.
- Many species use fatty-acid-derived Type-I sex pheromones.
- C18 2,13- and 3,13-diene derivatives recur prominently.
- Blend composition can be highly species-specific.
- Minor compounds can be essential synergists or antagonists.
- Closely related signals can differ by double-bond position, geometry, or terminal group.
- Good nuclear genomes now exist for some chemically characterized species.
- Functionally characterized moth FADs, FARs, and other pathway enzymes from other families provide comparative references.

## Weak / unresolved

- exact biosynthetic pathways for many Sesiidae pheromones;
- which Sesiidae genes encode the relevant specialized enzymes;
- direct versus elongation-derived origin of some double-bond positions;
- origin of unusual Δ2 / Δ3 unsaturation;
- exact substrate states used by the responsible enzymes;
- tissue-specific expression for many candidate genes;
- protein structures and substrate-bound geometries;
- how biosynthetic changes coevolve with male receptor preferences.

These gaps are not weaknesses of the project.

They are the research space.

# 31. Design principle for future writing

When describing this program publicly, keep three claims distinct.

### Historical/personal motivation

> Interest in *Amegilla* and bee taxonomy led to bee-mimicking Sesiidae and then to their chemical ecology.

### Ecological relationship

> Sesiidae commonly mimic Hymenoptera, including bee-like phenotypes and behaviors; *Amegilla* provides one comparative bee system.

### Biochemical comparison

> Both moth pheromone systems and some bee recognition systems recruit lipid-derived molecules for communication.

Do **not** turn these into unsupported claims that:

- Sesiidae evolved their pheromone system because they mimic *Amegilla*;
- a specific bee-mimicking Sesiidae species is proven to use a particular *Amegilla* species as its model;
- Sesiidae Type-I pheromones and *Amegilla* CHCs are the same biosynthetic signaling system.

# 32. Working big-picture hypothesis

The broader idea worth exploring is:

> **Lipid metabolism is not merely a source of membrane and storage molecules; across insects it repeatedly becomes an evolvable signaling substrate.**

Its modular chemistry makes it possible to encode identity using:

```text
chain length
position
geometry
functional group
mixture
timing
```

Sesiidae provide a particularly striking case because their pheromone space contains a compact family of closely related C18 structures with dramatically different behavioral meanings.

The Atlas can make that chemical space explicit and connect it to the genomes capable of producing it.

# 34. Final framing

The Sesiidae program began with a visual resemblance that raised a natural-history question.

But its deeper scientific value is that it opens a path from:

```text
what does this insect look like?
```

to:

```text
how does this insect know what it is?
```

The first question leads to mimicry and taxonomy.

The second leads to pheromones, lipid metabolism, enzymes, genomes, and molecular recognition.

That is why Sesiidae are such a compelling continuation of the *Amegilla* story.
